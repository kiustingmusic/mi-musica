STIs: understand, prevent and detect

Sexually transmitted infections can affect anyone who is sexually active. Many cause no symptoms, making prevention and appropriate testing essential parts of sexual health.

🛡️ What is a sexually transmitted infection?

A sexually transmitted infection (STI) is an infection caused by bacteria, viruses, parasites or other microorganisms that can be transmitted primarily through sexual contact.

Transmission may occur during vaginal, anal or oral sex. Some infections may also spread through intimate skin-to-skin contact, blood, shared injection equipment, or from mother to baby during pregnancy or childbirth.

A person can have an STI without knowing it. Many infections cause no symptoms or symptoms may be so mild that they go unnoticed. Having no symptoms does not rule out an STI.

🔬 Common sexually transmitted infections

Chlamydia

A bacterial infection that frequently causes no symptoms. It may affect the genital tract, rectum and other exposed sites. Untreated infection can lead to complications including pelvic inflammatory disease.

Gonorrhea

A bacterial infection that can affect the urethra, cervix, rectum and throat. It may cause discharge or painful urination but can also remain asymptomatic.

Syphilis

A bacterial infection that develops in stages. It may begin with a painless sore and later cause rash or other manifestations. Untreated infection can eventually affect the nervous system and other organs.

HIV

A virus that affects the immune system. Highly effective treatments can now control the infection. Testing allows people to know their status and access medical care early.

Human papillomavirus · HPV

A very common infection. Most infections cause no major problems, but some HPV types can cause genital warts while others are associated with several cancers.

Genital herpes

Usually caused by herpes simplex viruses. It can cause painful blisters or ulcers, although many people have mild or unrecognized symptoms.

Trichomoniasis

A parasitic infection that may cause discharge, irritation or painful urination. Many infected people have no symptoms.

Hepatitis B

It can spread through sexual contact and blood exposure. An effective vaccine is available to prevent hepatitis B.

Hepatitis C

It is primarily transmitted through blood exposure. Sexual transmission is less common but can occur in certain situations. There is currently no hepatitis C vaccine.

👀 Possible symptoms

Genital symptoms

Sores, blisters, warts, unusual discharge, itching, irritation or pain.

Urinary symptoms

Burning, pain or discomfort during urination may occur with some infections.

Pelvic symptoms

Pelvic pain, pain during sex or abnormal bleeding may require medical evaluation.

Mouth, throat and rectum

STIs can also affect these sites depending on sexual practices and the microorganism involved.

Skin

Some infections can cause rash, warts, ulcers or other skin lesions.

No symptoms

This is particularly important: feeling completely well does not necessarily mean that an infection is absent.

🧪 How are STIs diagnosed?

There is no single test that detects every STI. Testing depends on the infection, symptoms and which parts of the body were exposed.

Blood tests

Used for certain infections including HIV, syphilis and hepatitis.

Urine

Urine testing can identify certain bacterial infections in appropriate clinical situations.

Genital samples

Vaginal, cervical or urethral samples may be used to identify specific microorganisms.

Throat and rectal samples

After certain exposures, throat or rectal testing may be needed. A urine test alone may not evaluate these sites.

The appropriate tests and their timing depend on the exposure. Testing very soon after a recent exposure may require clinical assessment or repeat testing later.

🛡️ Reducing the risk

Condoms and barriers

Correct and consistent use of barrier methods substantially reduces the risk of many STIs.

Testing

Knowing your own and your partner's sexual health status can identify infections that cause no symptoms.

Vaccination

Vaccines are available to prevent HPV-related infections and hepatitis B. Hepatitis A vaccination is also recommended for certain groups.

Reducing exposure

Reducing the number of sexual partners and mutual monogamy with a tested partner can lower risk.

Do not share needles

Sharing needles or other injection equipment can transmit HIV and viral hepatitis.

Talk before sex

Discussing testing, prevention and sexual health before a new relationship can support informed decisions.

Important: birth control pills, implants, IUDs, patches, rings and other non-barrier contraceptive methods do not protect against STIs.

💊 HIV: PrEP and PEP are different

PrEP · before exposure

Pre-exposure prophylaxis is an HIV prevention strategy for people without HIV who may be exposed to the virus. It requires appropriate medical assessment and follow-up.

PEP · after possible exposure

Post-exposure prophylaxis is an emergency HIV prevention intervention after certain potential exposures and should be assessed as soon as possible.

🚨 Recent possible HIV exposure

If a possible HIV exposure has occurred, do not wait for symptoms.

  • Seek medical evaluation immediately.
  • PEP should be started as soon as possible when indicated.
  • The recommended maximum window to start PEP is 72 hours after exposure.
  • The earlier the exposure is assessed, the better.
  • Testing for other STIs and viral hepatitis may also be appropriate.

🧬 HPV, vaccination and cancer

Human papillomavirus is extremely common. Most infections do not cause major health problems, but some HPV types can cause genital warts and others are associated with cancers of the cervix, anus, penis, vulva, vagina and oropharynx.

Vaccination can prevent many HPV infections and their complications. The vaccine prevents new infections; it does not treat an infection that is already present.

Condoms reduce the risk of HPV and herpes but do not provide complete protection because these infections can involve skin or mucosal surfaces not covered by a condom.

🤰 STIs and pregnancy

Some STIs can affect pregnancy or be transmitted to a baby during pregnancy or childbirth. This is why certain STI tests are part of prenatal care.

CDC recommends testing for syphilis, HIV, hepatitis B and hepatitis C early in pregnancy, with additional or repeat testing when indicated.

⚠️ When should you seek medical care?

  • After a possible exposure to an STI.
  • New sores, blisters or lesions involving the genitals, mouth or anus.
  • New or abnormal genital discharge.
  • Pain or burning during urination.
  • Significant or persistent pelvic pain.
  • Rectal pain or discharge after sexual exposure.
  • An unexplained rash, particularly with other symptoms.
  • A sexual partner has been diagnosed with an STI.
  • Possible HIV exposure within the previous 72 hours.

❓ Frequently asked questions

Can I have an STI without symptoms?

Yes. Many STIs frequently cause no symptoms. Appropriate testing is the way to identify many of these infections.

Can oral sex transmit STIs?

Yes. Several STIs can be transmitted through oral sex and may infect the mouth or throat.

Does a urine test detect every STI?

No. Depending on the infection and exposure, blood tests or vaginal, urethral, throat, rectal or lesion samples may be necessary.

Do condoms provide 100% protection?

They greatly reduce the risk of many STIs when used correctly, but HPV, herpes and syphilis can sometimes involve skin that is not covered by a condom.

Does birth control protect against STIs?

Non-barrier contraception such as pills, IUDs, implants, patches and rings does not protect against sexually transmitted infections.

Are all STIs curable?

No. Many bacterial and parasitic infections can be cured with appropriate treatment. Some viral infections can be effectively controlled but may not be eliminated from the body.
