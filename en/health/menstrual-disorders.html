Menstrual disorders: understanding changes in your cycle

Menstrual patterns can change throughout life. Important changes in frequency, duration, bleeding volume or pain, however, may require medical evaluation.

🩸 Understanding the menstrual cycle

The menstrual cycle is counted from the first day of one period to the first day of the next. It is regulated through interactions between the brain, pituitary gland, ovaries and uterus.

Hormone levels change during the cycle, an ovarian follicle develops, ovulation may occur and the uterine lining prepares for a possible pregnancy. If pregnancy does not occur, the lining is shed as a period.

In adults, many clinical references consider cycles of approximately 21 to 35 days and menstrual bleeding lasting up to 7 days to be within a typical range. Age, contraception and reproductive stage can affect these patterns.

📅 Common menstrual problems

Irregular cycles

The interval between periods changes considerably or menstruation occurs at unexpected frequencies.

Heavy menstrual bleeding

The amount or duration of bleeding interferes with everyday activities or causes symptoms related to blood loss.

Amenorrhea

Absence of menstruation. It can occur naturally during pregnancy, breastfeeding and menopause but may also have other causes.

Dysmenorrhea

Pain associated with menstruation. It can be primary or related to an underlying gynecologic condition.

Bleeding between periods

Bleeding or spotting outside the expected menstrual period may require evaluation depending on age and circumstances.

Bleeding after sex

This may have cervical, vaginal or uterine causes and should be evaluated when new or recurrent.

🌊 Abnormal uterine bleeding

Abnormal uterine bleeding describes changes in the frequency, regularity, duration or amount of uterine bleeding.

It is a symptom rather than a single diagnosis. Causes may be hormonal, structural, hematologic, medication-related or associated with other medical conditions.

Fibroids

Benign growths of uterine muscle that can cause heavy or prolonged periods, pelvic pressure and pain.

Polyps

Usually benign growths involving the uterine lining or cervix that may cause irregular bleeding or spotting.

Adenomyosis

A condition involving endometrial tissue within the uterine muscle that may cause heavy and painful periods.

Ovulation problems

Irregular ovulation can lead to irregular or heavy uterine bleeding.

🩸 Heavy menstrual bleeding

Heavy menstrual bleeding can interfere with quality of life and may lead to iron deficiency or anemia.

  • Changing menstrual protection every one to two hours.
  • Bleeding for more than a week.
  • Needing double menstrual protection.
  • Waking at night to change protection.
  • Repeated large blood clots.
  • Fatigue, weakness, dizziness or shortness of breath.
A complete blood count and, when appropriate, iron studies can help identify anemia related to menstrual blood loss.

⏸️ Amenorrhea

Amenorrhea may be primary, when menstruation has not begun by the expected age, or secondary, when someone who previously menstruated stops having periods for several months.

Pregnancy

Pregnancy is an important possibility to exclude when a period is unexpectedly missed in someone who could become pregnant.

PCOS

Polycystic ovary syndrome can cause irregular ovulation, widely spaced periods or amenorrhea.

Thyroid and hormonal disorders

Thyroid disease, elevated prolactin and other endocrine disorders may affect menstruation.

Nutrition and intense exercise

Major weight loss, inadequate energy intake, eating disorders and intense physical training can interfere with ovulation.

Stress

Severe physical or emotional stress can temporarily affect the hormonal pathways controlling ovulation.

Contraception and medications

Some hormonal contraceptives and medications can change or suppress menstrual bleeding.

⚡ Painful periods · Dysmenorrhea

Mild menstrual cramps are common, but severe pain that prevents normal work, school, sleep or daily activities deserves evaluation.

Primary dysmenorrhea

Menstrual pain without an identified pelvic disease. Prostaglandins and uterine contractions play an important role.

Secondary dysmenorrhea

Pain caused by another condition such as endometriosis, adenomyosis, fibroids or another pelvic disorder.

Regular exercise, adequate sleep and local heat can help some people with menstrual discomfort. Severe or worsening pain should be investigated.

🌸 Endometriosis

Endometriosis occurs when tissue similar to the uterine lining grows outside the uterus. It may cause inflammation, pelvic pain and fertility problems.

  • Severe menstrual pain.
  • Pelvic pain that worsens over time.
  • Pain during or after sex.
  • Pain with bowel movements or urination around menstruation.
  • Heavy or irregular bleeding.
  • Difficulty becoming pregnant.

🧬 Polycystic ovary syndrome · PCOS

PCOS is a common endocrine disorder during reproductive years. It can interfere with ovulation and cause irregular or absent periods.

Cycle changes

Ovulation may occur less often, resulting in widely spaced periods or amenorrhea.

Androgen excess

Possible signs include acne, excess facial or body hair and androgen-pattern hair loss.

Metabolic health

Some people with PCOS have insulin resistance, abnormal glucose levels, dyslipidemia or other metabolic risk factors.

Fertility

Irregular ovulation can make pregnancy more difficult, but treatments are available when fertility is desired.

An ultrasound showing multiple ovarian follicles does not by itself diagnose PCOS. Diagnosis requires assessment of the overall clinical and hormonal picture.

🌙 PMS and PMDD

Premenstrual syndrome (PMS) involves recurring physical and emotional symptoms before menstruation that improve after the period begins.

Physical symptoms

Bloating, breast tenderness, headache, fatigue, appetite changes and muscle discomfort may occur.

Emotional symptoms

Irritability, mood changes, anxiety, difficulty concentrating and sleep changes may occur.

When mood symptoms are severe enough to substantially disrupt work, school, relationships or everyday life, premenstrual dysphoric disorder (PMDD) may need to be considered.

🌿 Healthy habits

Physical activity

Regular aerobic activity can support general health and may help reduce some menstrual and premenstrual symptoms.

Balanced nutrition

A varied diet containing vegetables, fruits, legumes, whole grains, protein and appropriate sources of iron and calcium supports health.

Sleep

Regular sleep habits may be particularly helpful when fatigue or premenstrual symptoms are present.

Stress management

Relaxation, breathing exercises, yoga or meditation may help some people manage symptoms.

🌅 Perimenopause

During the transition toward menopause, ovulation becomes less predictable and menstrual frequency and bleeding can change.

Although some variation is expected, very heavy bleeding, bleeding between periods, bleeding after sex or any bleeding after menopause should be evaluated.

Bleeding after menopause: after 12 consecutive months without menstruation, any new vaginal bleeding should be evaluated by a healthcare professional.

🔬 How are menstrual disorders evaluated?

Menstrual history

Timing, duration, frequency, amount of bleeding, clots, pain and changes from the usual pattern are important.

Pregnancy testing

This may be one of the first tests when pregnancy is possible and amenorrhea or abnormal bleeding occurs.

Blood count and iron

These can help identify anemia or iron deficiency when bleeding is heavy.

Hormonal testing

Depending on the situation, thyroid function, prolactin, androgens or other hormones may be evaluated.

Gynecologic ultrasound

Ultrasound can assess the uterus, endometrium and ovaries and help identify structural abnormalities.

Endometrial evaluation

Depending on age and risk factors, hysteroscopy, sonohysterography or endometrial biopsy may be appropriate.

📱 Track your menstrual cycle

Keeping records for several cycles can provide useful information for medical evaluation.

  • First and last day of bleeding.
  • Light, moderate or heavy flow.
  • How often menstrual protection is changed.
  • Blood clots.
  • Pain and its severity.
  • Bleeding between periods.
  • Bleeding after sex.
  • Premenstrual physical or emotional symptoms.
  • Medications and contraception.

🚨 Warning signs

  • Very heavy bleeding soaking a pad or tampon about every hour for several hours.
  • Heavy bleeding with dizziness, fainting, shortness of breath or chest pain.
  • Sudden severe pelvic pain.
  • Bleeding when pregnancy may be possible.
  • Any bleeding after menopause.
  • Period pain severe enough to prevent normal activities.
  • Previously regular periods becoming persistently irregular.
  • Unexplained absence of menstruation for several months.

❓ Frequently asked questions

Does a normal menstrual cycle have to be exactly 28 days?

No. Menstrual cycle length varies among individuals and can also change throughout life.

Is severe period pain normal?

Mild cramps are common, but severe pain that disrupts everyday activities should be evaluated.

Can heavy periods cause anemia?

Yes. Repeated blood loss may cause iron deficiency and anemia, which can lead to fatigue, weakness, dizziness or shortness of breath.

Can stress affect menstruation?

Yes, but persistent menstrual changes should not automatically be attributed to stress without considering other causes.

Does PCOS always mean ovarian cysts?

No. PCOS is a hormonal and ovulatory disorder, and ultrasound appearance alone does not establish the diagnosis.

Can contraception change menstrual bleeding?

Yes. Depending on the method, bleeding may become lighter, irregular or may stop. Copper IUDs may increase bleeding in some users, particularly at first.

Is bleeding after menopause normal?

No. Any bleeding after menopause should be medically evaluated.
