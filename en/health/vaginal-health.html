Vaginal Health

Knowing which changes may be normal and which ones deserve medical evaluation can help protect vaginal, sexual and reproductive health without relying on self-diagnosis.

What do we mean by vaginal health?

The vagina is the muscular canal extending from the vulva to the cervix. The vulva refers to the external genital structures. Although these terms are sometimes used interchangeably in everyday conversation, they are anatomically different.

The vagina has its own ecosystem of microorganisms. The balance of this microbiome, together with hormonal, sexual, immune and environmental factors, plays an important role in vaginal health.

Important: vaginal discharge does not automatically mean infection. A new or persistent change in color, odor, amount or consistency deserves attention, especially when accompanied by itching, burning, pain or bleeding.

Vaginal discharge: typical patterns and changes

Vaginal secretions are part of normal physiology. Their characteristics may vary with the menstrual cycle, sexual activity, pregnancy and hormonal changes.

It may be physiologic

Discharge without a strong unpleasant odor and without itching, burning, pain or other significant symptoms may be normal.

Consider evaluation

A marked change in odor, color, amount or consistency, particularly when accompanied by discomfort, may indicate vaginitis or another condition.

Itching or burning

These symptoms may be associated with infection, irritation, allergy, hormonal changes or certain skin conditions.

Unexpected bleeding

Bleeding between periods, after sex or after menopause should be evaluated by a health professional.

Vaginitis: different causes can look similar

Vaginitis is inflammation of the vagina. It can cause discharge, itching, irritation or pain. Symptoms may overlap even when the underlying causes are very different.

Bacterial vaginosis

This occurs when the usual balance of vaginal bacteria changes. It may cause thin white, gray or greenish discharge and a strong characteristic odor, although some people have no symptoms.

Vulvovaginal candidiasis

This is related to excessive growth of Candida, a yeast that may normally be present in the genital microbiome. It often causes itching, inflammation and sometimes thick white discharge.

Trichomoniasis

This is a sexually transmitted infection caused by a parasite. It may cause yellow-green discharge and irritation, but it can also occur without obvious symptoms.

Vulvovaginal candidiasis and bacterial vaginosis are not classically considered sexually transmitted infections. Trichomoniasis is an STI.

Other causes of vaginal symptoms

Chemical irritation

Scented products, vaginal douches, certain soaps and other irritants can cause burning, dryness or inflammation.

Hormonal changes

Lower estrogen levels, especially after menopause, can contribute to dryness, irritation and pain during sex.

Skin conditions

Some dermatologic disorders can affect the vulva and cause symptoms that resemble a vaginal infection.

Sexually transmitted infections

Chlamydia, gonorrhea, herpes and other STIs may also cause discharge, lesions, pain or bleeding and require specific testing.

How is the cause diagnosed?

Because different conditions may produce similar symptoms, identifying the cause based only on the appearance of discharge is not always reliable.

Medical history

Symptoms, duration, previous episodes, medications, menstruation, pregnancy, sexual activity and STI risk may all be relevant.

Examination

When appropriate, evaluation may include examination of the vulva, vagina and cervix.

Vaginal sample

Vaginal or cervical secretions may be tested to identify microorganisms or changes in the vaginal microbiome.

pH and additional testing

Vaginal pH can provide useful information, but pH alone does not necessarily establish the diagnosis.

Hospital Clínic Barcelona includes Gram-stained vaginal secretion samples among its microbiological methods for evaluating bacterial vaginosis.

Intimate hygiene: less can be more

Avoid vaginal douching

The vagina has natural cleaning mechanisms. Internal douching can disrupt the normal balance of vaginal bacteria.

Be careful with fragrances

Intimate deodorants and other scented products may irritate sensitive genital tissue.

Safer sex

Correct condom use helps reduce the risk of many sexually transmitted infections.

Sex toys

Clean them according to the manufacturer's instructions and avoid sharing them without appropriate hygiene or protection.

Vaginal dryness and menopause

Estrogen levels fall after menopause and may also decrease during breastfeeding or in certain medical situations. This can cause dryness, irritation, burning and pain during sex.

These symptoms do not have to be accepted as an inevitable part of aging. When they affect comfort, well-being or sexual health, they should be discussed with a health professional.

⚠️ When should you seek medical care?

  • A significant or persistent change in the color, odor or amount of discharge.
  • Severe or persistent itching, burning or irritation.
  • Pelvic pain, fever or chills.
  • Persistent pain during sex.
  • Bleeding between periods or after sex.
  • Any vaginal bleeding after menopause.
  • New genital sores, ulcers, blisters or lesions.
  • Symptoms after a new sexual partner or possible STI exposure.
  • Symptoms that continue after previous treatment or repeatedly return.

Frequently asked questions

Does all vaginal discharge mean infection?

No. Vaginal secretions may be physiologic. New changes in odor, color, amount or consistency deserve attention, particularly when itching, burning, pain or bleeding is also present.

Is a yeast infection a sexually transmitted infection?

It is not considered a classic STI. Candida may normally be present in the genital microbiome, and certain conditions can allow excessive growth.

Is bacterial vaginosis the same as a yeast infection?

No. Bacterial vaginosis involves a change in bacterial balance, while a yeast infection involves excessive Candida growth. Their treatments are also different.

Can odor alone tell me which infection I have?

Not reliably. Symptoms can provide clues, but different conditions may overlap and laboratory testing may be needed.

Should I douche to keep the vagina clean?

No. Vaginal douching can disrupt the natural microbial balance and may contribute to irritation or other problems.

Does vaginal dryness happen only after menopause?

No. It is common after menopause because of lower estrogen levels, but it can also occur during other stages of life and for different reasons.
