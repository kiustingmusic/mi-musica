My mother's Cuban cooking

In Cuba, a mother does not say "I love you" every five minutes: she cooks it. This is a tribute to that cooking — and the story of the 125-recipe book born so it would never be lost.

A cuisine built with whatever there was

Traditional Cuban cooking is the child of mixture and necessity: Spanish, African, and Caribbean roots forced to get along with whatever showed up at the market. What came out is a cuisine of depth, not display: the sofrito of onion, pepper, and garlic that opens almost every dish; the rice that is never missing; black beans rested overnight; ropa vieja that turns flank steak into a celebration; eastern congrí, grandmothers' ajiaco, the roast pork of New Year's Eve. Nothing is thrown away: today's broth is tomorrow's soup, and the leftover root vegetable comes back as a fritter.

That cooking was learned by watching, not by reading. Nobody measured "one cup": it was "a handful," "a little splash," "until it smells right." That is why so many emigrants discover, once far away, that they know the flavor by heart but cannot reproduce it. The flavor is there; the recipe is not.

Clara Elena, the cook of the house

In Kiustin's home, the kitchen was the kingdom of his mother, Clara Elena Rojas Rojas. As in thousands of Cuban homes, her way of loving was to put a plate on the table: with whatever there was, at whatever hour, for whoever arrived. Kiustin grew up in Banes watching humble ingredients become a celebration in her hands, and how the family table was the true center of the house — more than the television, more than any visitor.

Clara Elena died in Cuba on June 27, 2023, two months after her son arrived in the United States. He could not be there. And as happens to every emigrant who loses a mother from far away, among the things that hurt most there is one very concrete: the flavors nobody will ever repeat quite the same. His first song, "La Cama Vacía," was born from that loss. This book was born from the same one.

Why a book of 125 recipes

"The Great Book of Cuban Cooking" was built on a simple idea: that the recipes of the house should not die with the generation that kept them in their heads. It holds 125 recipes — appetizers, soups, rice dishes, meats, seafood, root vegetables, and desserts — written with clear measurements and ordered steps, so a son or granddaughter raised in Rochester, Miami, or Madrid can stand in their kitchen and reach their grandmother's flavor without guessing.

The book is dedicated to Clara Elena's memory, and that dedication is not a courtesy line: it is the project's engine. Behind every recipe sits the question thousands of Cuban families scattered across the world ask themselves: how do I pass this on to mine before it is lost?

Cooking Cuban outside Cuba

The diaspora changed the cooking without killing it. In Rochester, New York, Kiustin cooks with what he can find: yuca arrives frozen, ripe plantains must be waited for, cachucha peppers are sometimes replaced with sweet peppers, and sour orange with a mix of orange and lime. The secret he always repeats is the same one that works in Banes and anywhere else: a well-made sofrito, patience with the beans, and no fear of tasting and adjusting. Cuban cooking survived a century of scarcities; it also survives an American supermarket.

And there is a second generation watching: children and grandchildren born speaking English who know Cuba first through the table. For them, a written cookbook is a bridge — the way for "grandma's food" to stop being only a story they are told and become something they can make with their own hands.
