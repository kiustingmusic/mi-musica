How to read your dialysis labs

Every month you receive a sheet full of numbers. This guide explains, in clear language, what each value measures, why it matters, and what questions to ask when something is out of range.

Why they draw your blood so often

In hemodialysis, monthly labs are not paperwork: they are the treatment's dashboard. With them, the team adjusts your diet, medications, dialysis time, and dry weight. A patient who understands their numbers stops being a spectator and becomes part of the team: they spot trends, follow the diet better, and arrive at appointments with concrete questions instead of vague fears. You do not need to be a doctor to understand the essentials; you need someone to explain it once, calmly.

Hemoglobin: the energy in your blood

Hemoglobin is the protein that carries oxygen inside red blood cells. Healthy kidneys produce a hormone — erythropoietin — that orders the body to make those cells; when the kidney fails, production falls and anemia appears, which is very common on dialysis. That is why many patients receive stimulating medications and iron during treatment. Low hemoglobin feels like tiredness that sleep does not fix, shortness of breath with small efforts, and paleness. Teams usually work with individualized goals around 10 to 11.5 g/dL, following current clinical guidelines and each person's case.

Iron: ferritin and transferrin saturation

Without iron there is no hemoglobin, so two values are measured alongside it: ferritin, which reflects your iron stores, and transferrin saturation (TSAT), which shows how much iron is available in circulation. Interpreting them has a catch: ferritin also rises with inflammation and infections, so a high number does not always mean "plenty of iron." That is why the team reads both values together, and why it makes no sense to compare them with the ranges of a person without kidney disease.

Potassium: the number that protects the heart

Potassium is a mineral that healthy kidneys remove effortlessly. On dialysis it accumulates between sessions, and when it climbs too high it can disturb the heart's rhythm without clear warning symptoms. The general blood reference range is around 3.5 to 5.0–5.5 mEq/L, and timing matters: potassium usually reads highest right before the first treatment of the week, after the long gap without dialysis. Diet is the main tool: fruits, juices, beans, and "light" salt substitutes (which are often pure potassium) are the sources watched most closely. Phosphorus, calcium, and PTH: the bone trio

These three values are read together. Phosphorus accumulates when the kidney fails; the body responds by raising PTH (parathyroid hormone), which "borrows" calcium from the bones to balance the blood — and over the years that weakens the skeleton, stiffens blood vessels, and produces an unforgettable itch. Calcium completes the picture. Control rests on three legs: diet (watch for the hidden phosphorus in processed foods and colas), the binders taken with meals to trap phosphorus in the gut, and dialysis itself. If your phosphorus will not come down, the first honest question is about the binders: they only work with food, not after it.

Albumin: the nutrition marker

Albumin is a blood protein that works as a thermometer of nutritional status and inflammation. In dialysis patients it is one of the strongest predictors of long-term health, and teams aim to keep it in the upper range, generally above 4.0 g/dL. When it drops, it is almost never "one" problem: poor appetite, infections, excess fluid, or insufficient dietary protein are usually involved. That is why dialysis emphasizes eating quality protein at every meal, even though earlier stages of kidney disease may have limited it before.

Kt/V: is the dialysis enough?

Kt/V (and its relative, URR) measures the dialysis dose: how much waste was cleared in the session compared with your body's total water. KDOQI guidelines use a minimum delivered Kt/V of 1.2 per treatment on a three-times-a-week schedule as a reference, with a usual target of 1.4. A repeatedly low Kt/V means reviewing the vascular access, the effective treatment time, and whether sessions were shortened. Deep down, it is the number that answers the question: "Am I being dialyzed enough?"

Keep your own record

Advice from veteran patients: save your results month by month — in a notebook, as phone photos, or in an app — and watch the trend, not just the single number. A phosphorus that falls three months in a row is worth more than one perfect isolated value. And arrive at each review with two or three written questions about your own data.
