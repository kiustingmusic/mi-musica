Starting dialysis: what to expect and how to prepare

A clear, jargon-free guide to your first weeks of hemodialysis, written from the daily experience of working alongside patients who have been exactly where you are now.

The first day: less frightening than you imagine

Most patients arrive at their first treatment with the same feeling: fear of the unknown. The dialysis unit, seen for the first time, can be intimidating — rows of reclining chairs, machines with tubes and screens, alarms going off now and then. But within a few sessions that scene becomes routine, and the team caring for you becomes familiar faces who know your name, your weight, and even how you take your coffee.

A typical hemodialysis treatment lasts three to four hours, three times a week. When you arrive you are weighed, your blood pressure and temperature are taken, and the technician or nurse connects your vascular access to the machine. During treatment you can sleep, watch television, listen to music, or chat. When it ends, you are disconnected, your blood pressure is confirmed stable, and you are weighed again: the difference between the two weights is the fluid the machine removed.

What the machine does, in plain words

When the kidneys can no longer do their job, waste products (such as urea and potassium) and extra fluid build up in the blood. The dialysis machine passes your blood through a special filter — the dialyzer — which removes those wastes and the excess fluid, then returns the cleaned blood to your body. It is not a new kidney and it does not cure kidney disease: it is a treatment that replaces part of that function for the length of the session. That is why consistency matters so much: skipping treatments or cutting them short leaves waste accumulated, and you feel it as fatigue, swelling, and shortness of breath.

Vascular access: your lifeline

For blood to reach the machine, an access is needed. The most recommended long-term option is the fistula: a surgical connection between an artery and a vein in the arm that, over several weeks, strengthens and allows repeated needle sticks. Another option is a graft (a synthetic tube under the skin) and, in temporary or urgent situations, a catheter in the neck or chest.

Care for your access like what it is: your lifeline. Do not let anyone take your blood pressure or draw blood from the fistula arm, do not carry heavy loads with that arm, check daily that you can feel the vibration (the "thrill") when you touch it, and report right away any redness, pain, bleeding, or if the vibration stops. If you have a catheter, keep it dry and covered at all times: shower carefully without wetting it, and no swimming pools.

Dry weight and fluids: the daily balance

Your team will set your dry weight: the weight at which your body holds no extra fluid and your blood pressure is under control. Between treatments, everything you drink and much of what you eat accumulates, because the kidney no longer removes it. Gaining too much between sessions forces the machine to remove a lot of fluid in a short time, and that usually translates into cramps, blood pressure drops, and feeling unwell during treatment.

The general advice in kidney education materials is to watch the salt — because salt makes you thirsty — rather than obsessing over water alone. Tricks veteran patients use: small cups, ice or sugar-free candies for a dry mouth, and weighing yourself at home if your team recommends it. If you notice shortness of breath when lying down, swelling that does not go down, or very rapid weight gain, do not wait for your next session to mention it.

Eating on dialysis: potassium, phosphorus, and protein

The kidney diet is not a list of eternal prohibitions; it is a balance you learn. Three names will come up every month in your labs: potassium, phosphorus, and albumin. High potassium affects the heart; high phosphorus damages bones and causes itching; low albumin signals malnutrition, which is why on dialysis — unlike in earlier stages of kidney disease — you need to eat enough quality protein. Your renal dietitian is the right person to adjust all this to your labs and your tastes; on this same site you will find a free potassium and phosphorus guide to get started, and a companion article on how to read your labs.

What is normal — and what you should not silently endure

It is common to feel tired on treatment day, to have an occasional cramp, or a blood pressure dip if fluid is removed quickly. What you should not normalize: shortness of breath, chest pain, access bleeding that will not stop, fever with a catheter, or dizziness that keeps you from getting home. During the session, speak up in the moment — that is why the team checks your vital signs regularly — and outside the clinic, call or seek care according to severity. Speaking up in time is part of the treatment.

Questions for your first week

Take these questions to your team; no informed patient is ever a burden: What is my dry weight, and how much can I gain between sessions? What type of access do I have and how do I care for it? Which of my medications change with dialysis, and when do I take them on treatment days? Who is my dietitian, and when is my first appointment? What do I do if I cannot come one day? How and when will I see my monthly labs? Write the answers down: a lot of information arrives in the first week, and forgetting half of it is normal.

Starting dialysis changes your weekly schedule, but it does not stop your life. Thousands of people work, travel, celebrate birthdays, and keep making plans with this treatment as part of their week. Understanding what is being done to you — and why — is the first step to carrying it with your head held high.
