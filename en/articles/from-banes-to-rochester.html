From Banes to Rochester: the story of Kiustin G

A small-town doctor who crossed the world with one suitcase and a notebook of songs, and who today works in dialysis by day and sings his people's stories by night.

The kid from Banes

Kiustin was born on July 6, 1972, in Banes, Holguín, a town in eastern Cuba where everyone knows you by your name and your family. Like so many small-town kids with more curiosity than resources, he found in education his first open door: he trained as a physician at the Nicaragua and Materno Infantil hospitals in his own Banes, and completed clinical rotations through his fifth year at Lenin Hospital in Holguín. His path in medicine was broad and hands-on: ultrasound and obstetric Doppler, pediatrics, orthopedics, gynecology, and minor surgery. In Cuba, a small-town doctor does not pick just one thing: he treats whatever walks through the door.

Two things from those years never left him: the craft of caring for people, and the habit of listening to stories. Every patient brought an entire life into the exam room, and Kiustin learned that behind every diagnosis there is a family, a fear, and a hope. Years later, that same listening would become songs.

The departure and the hardest blow

In 2023 came the moment that marks every emigrant: the goodbye. Kiustin left Cuba by plane, through the normal airport, his heart split between hope for what was ahead and grief for what stayed behind. He settled in Rochester, New York, to start from zero in a new country, with a language he was still learning.

Two months after his arrival came the blow no emigrant child stops fearing: his mother, Clara Elena Rojas Rojas, died in Cuba on June 27, 2023. He lived it from far away, as so many must. The night she died, she came to say goodbye to him in a dream, moments before his sister Melvis called at 4 a.m. with the news. That wound — the mother who leaves without the last embrace — remains the emotional center of his music to this day.

Starting over: dialysis

In the United States, his Cuban degree does not allow him to practice as a physician, and Kiustin has never pretended otherwise: he presents himself as what he is. He works as a dialysis technician (PCT) at a Fresenius clinic in Greece, New York, where every week he cares for patients who depend on a machine to live — and where his years of clinical experience show in every shift. In November 2026 he was approved as a Water Preceptor, the technician responsible for dialysis water quality and for training others — a recognition he earned quietly, shift by shift.

From that daily work also came his tools for patients: the Interpreta tus Labs app, the free potassium and phosphorus guide, and a study program for dialysis technicians. His rule is simple and he keeps it: all the health content he publishes is educational, original, and based on public standards, without promising what he cannot promise.

Kiustin G, "El Doctor del Ritmo," is born

His first song as Kiustin G was "La Cama Vacía" ("The Empty Bed"), and it could not have been any other: it is his mother's song. Then came the takeoff nobody handed him. In October 2026, "La Utopía Camina" — a rhythmic satire about today's Cuba — passed 150 thousand views on Facebook and gave him his first large audience. His programming rule ever since is one he discovered himself: stories with a face and a heart are the ones that grow.

In November 2026, a photo of the Banes hospital in ruins touched his deepest nerve, and that night he wrote "El Hospital Donde Me Formé" ("The Hospital Where I Trained"), the town's son singing to the hospital that made him a doctor. You can read that story and others in the Stories section, and the full lyrics in Lyrics.

Books, cooking, and family

The creativity did not stop at music. He published "El Gran Libro de la Cocina Cubana" ("The Great Book of Cuban Cooking"), a cookbook of 125 recipes dedicated to his mother's memory — because in his home, as in so many Cuban homes, the kitchen was the language of love. He also published his Yoruba religion book "Ebbós, Ofrendas y Adimús a los Orichas" — Kiustin is a son of Obatalá Ayaguna and Naná Burucú, and his faith is part of his identity — and his story-and-song novel "La sala donde el reloj aprendió a escuchar" ("The Room Where the Clock Learned to Listen"), where the people of his clinic and his life cross paths between stories.

In Rochester, his family is with him: his partner Dalkis, and his children and grandchildren on both sides of the sea — Samuel, 14, is still in Cuba and writes to him every day. Distance, he says, is not cured: it is sung, cooked, and worked. That is why his motto is not decoration. It is literally his life: true stories, told with rhythm.
